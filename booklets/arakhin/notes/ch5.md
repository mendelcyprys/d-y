# intro
Perek 5 covers unusual vows: vows of **weight**, vows on **limbs** ("the value of my hand", "the erech of my head"), and vows of **half** ("half my erech" vs "the erech of half of me"). It explains what happens when the pledger or the person valued dies, and ends with collecting by **taking pledges** (*mashkonos*). The guiding contrast is between *erech*, a fixed sum due for a whole living person (ערך **נפשות**), and *damim*, a market price that has to be assessed.

# 5:1
**"משקלי עלי"**, "my weight is upon me". If he said silver, he gives his weight in silver. If gold, in gold. The Mishnah's lesson (Bartenura) is that without specifying, he can give his weight in **anything sold by weight in that place**, even pitch or onions. Yachin and Lechem Mishneh add that this is only when he says he meant it so. Otherwise an unspecified vow is taken strictly. The Gemara reads the Mishnah as missing a clause (*chasurei mechasra*): a **wealthy** person is judged by his standing even without specifying. This is where the story fits of the mother of **Yirmatya**. She vowed her daughter's weight, went up to Yerushalayim, and gave it in **gold**, because she was known to be very rich (Bartenura, Rambam). Rambam adds a parallel case. "קומתי עלי" (my height) means a rod of his full height. If he did not specify the material, his wealth decides.

**"משקל ידי עלי"**, "the weight of my hand". You cannot weigh a hand that is attached, since a man can tense or relax it. **R' Yehudah:** fill a barrel with water and put the arm in up to the **elbow**. For vows we follow ordinary speech, and "yad" then meant the forearm. Then make up the displaced water with donkey meat, sinews and bones, because "their flesh is as the flesh of donkeys" (Yechezkel 23:20, a *zecher ledavar*). Weigh that. Tosafos rules the meat must be weighed **before** going into the water, which it absorbs. Tosafos Yom Tov explains how Rambam's order can still work. **R' Yosei:** how can you match flesh to flesh and bone to bone? Instead we **estimate** how much the hand would weigh. The halachah follows R' Yosei.

# 5:2
**"דמי ידי עלי"**, "the value of my hand". We do not estimate him as if the hand were cut off, because a maimed man sells cheaply for being disfigured. Instead he is valued **as a slave whose master sold him except for one hand**. That hand keeps working (writing) for the first master. The difference between his price with the hand and without it goes to hekdesh (Gemara, Bartenura, Rambam).

**Stricter in *nedarim* (*damim*):** "the value of my hand" is a valid vow. "**The *erech* of my hand**" means nothing, because the Torah gives an *erech* only to a whole living person (ערך **נפשות**; Tosafos Yom Tov, from the Gemara's reading of בערכך). **Unless it is an organ life depends on.** "The *erech* of my head" or "of my liver" means the full *erech*. "זה הכלל" adds any such organ, for example an injury from the knee up (Gemara). Yachin explains the choice of examples: the head is the chief external organ and the liver an internal one.

**Stricter in *arachin*:** someone who said "ערכי עלי" and died has his heirs pay, because the *erech* is a fixed debt. Bartenura and Rambam say this applies only if he already **"stood in judgment"** before death, from והעמידו לפני הכהן. Tosafos Yom Tov asks why that verse is relevant, since it speaks only of a poor man. Ra'avad says the "standing in judgment" in the Gemara is simply about a debt becoming collectible from heirs. He adds that now that oral debts are collected from heirs, it is not needed at all. Tosafos Yom Tov answers that this practice is a Geonic enactment, which does not reach every case.

"דמי עלי" and he died: the heirs do **not** pay, because "**אין דמים למתים**". His vow was only for what beis din *would* assess, and a dead man cannot be assessed as a slave (Rashi). Tosafos Yom Tov suggests the reason is that a corpse is forbidden for benefit. **Yachin** rejects this firmly: "Do they assess his flesh while he is alive?!"

# 5:3
**"חצי ערכי עלי"** ("half my erech") gives half the *erech*. "**ערך חציי עלי**" ("the erech of half of me") gives the **whole** *erech*, because a man cannot live without half his body. The same holds for *damim*: נדר בערכך נפשות compares *neder* to *erech* (Bartenura). Tosafos Yom Tov explains why the comparison does not work in reverse. "The *erech* of my hand" is excluded by בערכך. But a hand does have a market value, so "the value of my hand" is not excluded.

**The second "זה הכלל"** extends the rule to "half of an organ life depends on", for example "**half** my heart" or "half my liver" (Bartenura, Rambam). Tosafos Yom Tov notes that Rambam leaves this case out of his code. He suggests Rambam took the second "זה הכלל" as simply repeating the first, and not as adding a new case.

# 5:4
**One person pledges another's *erech*.** If either one dies, the heirs pay, provided the one valued had stood in judgment. If **the one valued** dies before that, the pledger owes nothing, because a dead man has no *erech* (Rambam, Hilchos Arachin end of ch. 1). **One person pledges another's *damim*.** If the **pledger** dies, his heirs pay. If **the one vowed about** dies, they do not, because "אין דמים למתים". The Gemara explains why the first clause is needed. One might think the pledger's property was never bound, since he died before the assessment. The Mishnah teaches that the later assessment only *reveals* a debt that existed from the time of the vow (Bartenura). Tosafos Yom Tov suggests Rambam did not see this as a separate teaching, and so did not quote it.

# 5:5
**An animal or house that is lost.** If he said "**this ox is an *olah***" or "**this house is *korban***" (for *bedek habayis*) and the ox died or the house collapsed, he owes nothing. He consecrated only *this* object. If he said "**the value of this ox is upon me** for an *olah*", he must pay. The word **עלי** makes him responsible, "as if he carried it on his shoulder". "אין דמים למתים" applies **only to people**: a dead ox is simply assessed at what it is now worth (Rambam). Tosafos and Yachin show from the Gemara that both clauses deal with consecrating for *value*. The only difference is whether he said עלי. Rashi adds that if he consecrated the animal itself, "this ox **upon me** is an *olah*" only makes him responsible for the effort of bringing it.

# 5:6
**Taking pledges.** For *arachin* (and *damim*), the **gizbar** may enter the debtor's house and take collateral against his will. For *chata'os* and *ashamos* he may not, since people bring those quickly for their own atonement. For *olos* and *shelamim* he may, since those are not seen as urgent. Exceptions: a **nazir's *chatas*** does not hold back his permission to drink wine (Bartenura stresses wine). Some delay it, so we take pledges. The **metzora's *olah*** holds back his purity, so we do not. Tosafos Yom Tov and Yachin explain why a defiled nazir's *asham* is not an exception.

**לרצונו** (Vayikra 1:3), "of his own will": we compel him **until he says "רוצה אני"**. The same is true of a **get**: when beis din compels a husband, the get is valid once he says "I want". Rav Sheshes deduces from "until he *says*" that merely handing it over is not enough, since he may have made a secret protest (*moda'ah*). Yachin explains the verse's wording: לרצון, not ברצון. Even consent pushed by fear of the lash counts as a turning toward his own will. Rambam develops this idea in his famous explanation in Hilchos Gerushin 2:20: the Jew's true self wants to do the mitzvah.
