# intro
The first perek sets out the four roles that run through the whole masechta. The **ma'arich** (מעריך) says "ערך פלוני עלי" or "ערכי עלי" and must pay the Torah's fixed *erech* of the person named. That person is the **ne'erach** (נערך). The **noder** (נודר) says "דמי פלוני עלי" and must pay that person's *damim*, what he would fetch on the market as a slave (Bartenura). That person is the **nidar** (נידר). The *erech* depends only on age and sex (Vayikra 27:3–7). The *damim* depends on the person himself. Unless the vower specified otherwise, all *arachin* and *damim* go to *bedek habayis*, the Temple upkeep fund (Tosafos Yom Tov, from Rambam, Hilchos Arachin 1).

# 1:1
**"הכל" — whom it comes to include.** The Gemara reads each "הכל" as adding a case. *Hakol ma'arichin* adds the **mufla samuch le'ish**: a boy just before adulthood whose vows take effect once he shows he understands to whom he vowed. The verse says איש כי יפליא. A boy close to bar mitzvah counts as *ish* for this purpose. Bartenura defines this boy as "thirteen and a day without two hairs". Tosafos Yom Tov argues the reading should be **twelve and a day**, as in Tosafos and the Mishnah in Niddah 5:6. Yachin says the word covers both a twelve-year-old with *simanim* and a thirteen-year-old without them, since neither is fully an adult but both can "*hafli*".

*Hakol ne'erachin* adds someone who is disfigured or covered in boils. He is worth nothing on the market but still has a full *erech*. *Erech* is not a price, and the rule is "נפשות כל דהו".

**Kohanim.** One might have thought a kohen is not subject to *arachin*, since the poor man is "stood before the kohen" (והעמידו לפני הכהן) and a kohen cannot stand before himself. Rambam gives a different reason: kohanim and leviim are exempt from *pidyon haben*, which is also measured "בערכך", so one might have thought they are exempt here too. The word איש in the parshah tells us otherwise.

**Women and slaves** own nothing of their own: a wife's earnings belong to her husband and a slave's to his master. So they pay when she is widowed or divorced, or when he is freed. They also pay if someone gave them money on condition that the husband or master has no rights to it (Tosafos, quoted in Tosafos Yom Tov and Yachin). A Canaanite slave is valued as a **male** even though for mitzvos he is treated like a woman (Tosafos Yom Tov).

**Tumtum and androgynos.** They can pledge someone else's *erech* and can vow or be the subject of *damim*. They cannot be valued, because the parshah says זכר and נקבה, which requires a definite male or a definite female.

**Cheresh, shoteh, katan.** They have no *da'as*, so their own vows mean nothing. Others can still pledge their value. **Less than a month old:** the parshah begins at one month, so there is no *erech*. There are still *damim*, because even a newborn has some market value.

# 1:2
**The nochri.** The parshah opens "דבר אל **בני ישראל**", which excludes him, and goes on "**איש** כי יפליא", which includes him. Each Tanna assigns the inclusion to one role and the exclusion to the other. **R' Meir** says he can be valued but cannot pledge. He reasons that the Torah is broader with *ne'erachin*, since a cheresh, shoteh and katan can be valued even though they cannot pledge. **R' Yehudah** says he can pledge but cannot be valued. He reasons from tumtum and androgynos, who pledge but are not valued. Yachin puts the difference this way: R' Meir compares the nochri to the cheresh, shoteh and katan, whose lack is in *da'as*. R' Yehudah compares him to the tumtum, whose lack is physical (no *milah* or *tevilah*).

**The halachah.** Bartenura and the printed Rambam commentary rule like R' Yehudah, by the usual rule. Tosafos Yom Tov points to the other side. The Gemara says R' Meir's view "is more reasonable", because of Ezra 4:3, "לא לכם ולנו לבנות בית": the Temple does not accept a nochri's gift for *bedek habayis* (Shekalim 1:5). Tosafos Yom Tov found that an accurate manuscript of Rambam's commentary reads "the halachah follows R' Meir". Rambam rules that way in Hilchos Arachin 1. Ra'avad objects on the basis of the general rule, and Kesef Mishneh defends Rambam.

**"Both agree they vow and are vowed."** By Rambam's ruling, a nochri who says "דמי עלי" must pay, but the money does **not** go into the Temple chamber. We find out what he intended. If he meant it the way a Jew would, beis din uses the money as it sees fit, but not for *bedek habayis* or *bedek Yerushalayim*. If he says "I vowed it to Heaven", the money is put away (*nignaz*) (Tosafos Yom Tov).

# 1:3
**The goses** is someone in the last throes of dying. Rambam describes it as "the sound of his throat heard at death". Tosafos Yom Tov connects the word to מגיס בקדירה, stirring a pot, because the phlegm churns in his throat. Yachin also discusses where the word comes from. The goses has no *damim*, because most *gosesim* die and nobody would buy him. He has no *erech* either, because the verse says והעמידו... והעריך, implying someone who can be "stood up" and has some permanence. Tosafos Yom Tov asks why this verse doesn't also exclude a lame person. He answers with Tosafos: since the Torah gave an *erech* to a one-month-old baby, it cannot require literal standing.

**One going out to be executed** by a Jewish beis din has no *damim*, because no one buys a man about to die. He has no *erech* because of כל חרם אשר יחרם מן האדם לא יפדה (Vayikra 27:29): a person "devoted" to death has no redemption. Someone condemned by the **king** is different. Kings sometimes relent, so he is valued and vowed as usual (Rambam). **R' Chanina ben Akavya** says he does have an *erech*, since it is fixed and does not depend on what he is worth. He uses the verse for something else: unlike the owner of the *shor mu'ad*, someone executed by beis din cannot buy his way out with *kofer*.

**R' Yosei.** Everyone agrees that the condemned man can vow, pledge and consecrate. They disagree about **damages** he caused. R' Yosei holds a debt "written in the Torah" is like one written in a *shtar*, so it can be collected from his heirs. The Tanna Kamma holds it is like an oral loan, which cannot be. The halachah follows the Tanna Kamma. In practice, though, an oral loan *is* collected from heirs, so beis din does collect the damages from his estate. Bartenura attributes this to a later enactment (*takanah*). Rambam simply calls it the accepted halachah. Tosafos Yom Tov asks why Bartenura speaks of a *takanah* at all. He suggests the enactment is the Geonim's extension of collection to the heirs' movable property.

# 1:4
**The pregnant woman sentenced to death.** We do not wait for the birth. One might think the fetus is "the husband's property" (כאשר ישית עליו בעל האשה) and should not be destroyed. But the verse says ומתו **גם** שניהם, and "גם" includes the fetus. Once she is **on the birthstool**, meaning labour has begun, the child has started to come out and counts as a separate body, so we wait. Tosafos Yom Tov stresses that the case is **after** the verdict. That is the only time delay counts as *inui hadin*, the forbidden prolonging of a condemned person's suffering. Boaz takes this to imply that before the verdict we do postpone the trial until she gives birth.

**Her hair.** Bartenura follows Rava. "Her hair" means a **wig** of another woman's hair tied to hers, and only if she said "give it to my daughter". That shows she did not want it treated as part of her body. Otherwise it is forbidden like any adornment of the dead. Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak reads it as her own hair, cut between the verdict and the execution. For a person only **death** forbids benefit, unlike an animal, which is forbidden from the moment of the **verdict**. Rambam (end of Hilchos Sanhedrin 12, and Hilchos Avel) permits benefit from an executed woman's own hair. Yachin explains his reason: hair is not changed by death. There is a long discussion of this in Tosafos Yom Tov, the Tur and Shulchan Aruch Yoreh De'ah 349, and the Rashba.

**An animal killed by beis din**, such as the stoned ox, is forbidden for any benefit, including its hair (Bartenura). This is learned from ובעל השור נקי. Tosafos Yom Tov points out that Bartenura's "even its hair" comes from Rashi on the *baraisa*, which fits Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak's reading rather than Rava's.
